Actions Are Judged by Intentions

Niyyah and the conditions of an accepted deed

A simple explanation of “Actions are only by intentions”, the first hadith in Imam an-Nawawi's Forty Hadith. Learn what niyyah (intention) really means, how intention turns everyday habits into worship, the difference between sincerity and showing off (riya'), the conditions for a deed to be accepted by Allah, and whether you should say your intention out loud. Includes an introduction to Imam an-Nawawi and the story of his famous book. The hadith is recited in Arabic, followed by a translation of its meaning.

The hadith

عَنْ أَمِيرِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ أَبِي حَفْصٍ عُمَرَ بْنِ الْخَطَّابِ رَضِيَ اللهُ عَنْهُ قَالَ: سَمِعْتُ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ صلى الله عليه وسلم يَقُولُ: " إنَّمَا الْأَعْمَالُ بِالنِّيَّاتِ، وَإِنَّمَا لِكُلِّ امْرِئٍ مَا نَوَى، فَمَنْ كَانَتْ هِجْرَتُهُ إلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ فَهِجْرَتُهُ إلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ، وَمَنْ كَانَتْ هِجْرَتُهُ لِدُنْيَا يُصِيبُهَا أَوْ امْرَأَةٍ يَنْكِحُهَا فَهِجْرَتُهُ إلَى مَا هَاجَرَ إلَيْهِ". رَوَاهُ إِمَامَا الْمُحَدِّثِينَ أَبُو عَبْدِ اللهِ مُحَمَّدُ بنُ إِسْمَاعِيل بن إِبْرَاهِيم بن الْمُغِيرَة بن بَرْدِزبَه الْبُخَارِيُّ الْجُعْفِيُّ رَضِيَ اللهُ عَنْهُمَا فِي "صَحِيحَيْهِمَا" اللذَينِ هُمَا أَصَحُّ الْكُتُبِ الْمُصَنَّفَةِ.

I heard the Messenger of Allah (ﷺ) say: "Actions are (judged) by motives (niyyah), so each man will have what he intended. Thus, he whose migration (hijrah) was to Allah and His Messenger, his migration is to Allah and His Messenger; but he whose migration was for some worldly thing he might gain, or for a wife he might marry, his migration is to that for which he migrated."

Reported by al-Bukhari and Muslim — an-Nawawi's Forty Hadith, no. 1

Before we start

Assalamu alaikum, I'm Heba. If this is your first time here: I'm summarizing Imam an-Nawawi's Forty Hadith, one hadith at a time, using mind maps and simple explanations, for anyone who doesn't have time for long lessons. And remember: what cannot be done completely should not be left completely. So let's begin: hadith number one.

Imagine two people praying side by side, the exact same prayer. One of them has a huge reward with Allah, and the other gets nothing at all! The difference isn't in the movements. It's in something no one can see: the intention. And that's our first hadith, and the most important one in the book.

Before we start, let's renew our intentions. The first hadith in the book is about sincerity, so the first thing we act on is the hadith itself. Why are you here to learn? And for whom?

Imam an-Nawawi

So who is Imam an-Nawawi? His name is Yahya ibn Sharaf an-Nawawi, a great scholar from Syria. He's called an-Nawawi after his hometown, Nawa. He passed away in the year 676 after the Hijra, when he was only forty-five years old!

And his books were blessed in an amazing way: the Forty Hadith, Riyad as-Salihin, and the book of daily remembrances, al-Adhkar. His commentary on Sahih Muslim is the most famous one. And he wrote a huge book of Islamic law, al-Majmu', but he passed away before finishing it, and later scholars completed it.

And this teaches us something important. In his time, there were scholars who knew more, wrote more, or lived longer, but the blessing was in his books. Why? Because we don't reach Allah by quantity, we reach Him by quality: by truthfulness, sincerity, and God-consciousness.

Some people attacked Imam an-Nawawi because of some interpretations he made in matters of Allah's attributes. Some even said his books should be burned! Shaykh Ibn Uthaymin replied: he was a scholar who did his best, and such scholars can be right or wrong. His mistakes are tiny compared to all the good he left, and we hope they are forgiven.

The book & its method

The book's original title is: The Forty, on the Foundations of Islam and the Principles of its Rulings. In other words, Imam an-Nawawi collected the hadiths that Islam revolves around, each one giving you a general principle with many branches under it.

In the introduction, Imam an-Nawawi mentions a narration saying that whoever preserves forty hadiths for the Muslims will be raised on the Day of Judgment among the scholars. But he himself said the hadith experts agree it's a weak narration! So why did he, and so many scholars, write collections of forty?

His answer: I don't rely on that weak narration. I rely on authentic ones, like the Prophet's words, peace be upon him: Let the one who is present convey to the one who is absent. And: May Allah brighten the face of a person who heard my words, understood them, and passed them on as he heard them. So we're conveying from the Prophet anyway, forty or fifty.

And here's a note: Imam an-Nawawi said scholars agree you can act on a weak hadith for virtuous deeds, but in fact they didn't all agree. Some rejected it completely, like Yahya ibn Ma'in, al-Bukhari, and al-Albani, saying the authentic hadith is enough for us. But most scholars allowed it, only for virtues, never for beliefs or rulings.

And those who allowed it set three conditions. First: the weakness must be mild, not severe. Second: it must fall under a principle that's already established, like charity, which is proven in authentic texts. Third: you don't believe the Prophet actually said it, so you never attribute to him what he didn't say.

For example: a weak hadith about the virtue of night prayer? That's fine, because there are many authentic hadiths about night prayer. But a weak hadith about a special prayer on the first night of Rajab, or the night of al-Isra? No, because there's no authentic basis for that at all.

His method in the book: First, every hadith is a major principle of Islam. Second, he committed to authentic hadiths, most of them from al-Bukhari and Muslim, the two most authentic collections. Third, he removed the long chains of narrators and kept only the companion's name, to make them easy to memorize. He did all that so you'd memorize them, so don't let him down!

And the story of the book: Imam Ibn as-Salah collected twenty-six hadiths about the principles of Islam. Imam an-Nawawi loved the idea and added sixteen, making forty-two. Later, Ibn Rajab added eight more, making fifty, and explained them in a great book called Jami' al-Ulum wal-Hikam.

The hadith

عَنْ أَمِيرِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ أَبِي حَفْصٍ عُمَرَ بْنِ الْخَطَّابِ رَضِيَ اللهُ عَنْهُ قَالَ: سَمِعْتُ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ صلى الله عليه وسلم يَقُولُ: " إنَّمَا الْأَعْمَالُ بِالنِّيَّاتِ، وَإِنَّمَا لِكُلِّ امْرِئٍ مَا نَوَى

From the Commander of the Believers, Abu Hafs Umar ibn al-Khattab, may Allah be pleased with him, who said: I heard the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, say: Actions are only by intentions, and every person will have only what they intended.

فَمَنْ كَانَتْ هِجْرَتُهُ إلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ فَهِجْرَتُهُ إلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ، وَمَنْ كَانَتْ هِجْرَتُهُ لِدُنْيَا يُصِيبُهَا أَوْ امْرَأَةٍ يَنْكِحُهَا فَهِجْرَتُهُ إلَى مَا هَاجَرَ إلَيْهِ

So whoever's migration was for Allah and His Messenger, their migration is for Allah and His Messenger. And whoever's migration was for some worldly gain, or to marry a woman, their migration is for what they migrated for.

The narrator is Umar ibn al-Khattab, the second caliph, known as al-Faruq, the one through whom Allah separated truth from falsehood. He said: I heard, meaning he heard it directly from the Prophet, peace be upon him. And it's reported by al-Bukhari and Muslim.

What intention does

Point one: how important is this hadith? Imam Ahmad said it's a third of Islam, and Imam ash-Shafi'i said it enters into seventy chapters of Islamic law, because intention is part of purification, prayer, charity, marriage, divorce, and almost everything. That's why Imam al-Bukhari opened his famous collection with it.

Point two: why was it said? It's reported that a man migrated to Medina to marry a woman called Umm Qays. The story itself is authentic, but linking it to this hadith isn't proven. And even if it were the reason, the rule is: what counts is the general wording, not the specific occasion.

Point three: what is intention, or niyyah in Arabic? It's the purpose and will behind an action, whether you're able to do it or not. With it, we tell actions apart: obligatory or voluntary? The noon prayer or the afternoon prayer? And with it, we know the goal of an action: who are you doing it for?

That's why, when jurists say intention is a condition of prayer, they mean defining the action, like: this is the noon prayer. But when books on purifying the soul say intention is everything, they mean sincerity: doing it for Allah.

Point four: intention does four things for us. It defines the purpose of the action: sincerity or showing off. It separates worship from habit. It separates acts of worship from each other. And it turns everyday permissible things into worship. Let's take them one by one.

First, the purpose. Say I was about to give five dollars to someone asking for help, and suddenly I saw people I know walking by, so I gave twenty instead! Now a question comes in: am I doing this for Allah, or for the people? That's the difference between sincerity and showing off.

Second, worship versus habit. Someone takes a shower every morning out of habit, then afterwards realizes he needed the ritual bath, the ghusl. Does he repeat it? Yes, because that shower was a habit, not a ghusl with the intention of purification. Same with someone who doesn't eat from dawn to sunset for a diet: that's a habit, not a fast.

Third, acts of worship from each other. You gave a hundred dollars to a poor person as charity, then found out you owed zakat, the obligatory alms. You can't count it as zakat, because your intention then was charity. Or two people enter the mosque before dawn and pray two units: one intends the Sunnah prayer, the other thinks it's the obligatory dawn prayer. Same prayer, but the intention made them different.

Fourth, intention turns the permissible into worship. Your sleep, so you can wake up for dawn prayer, can become worship. Your marriage, if you intend to follow the Sunnah and protect yourself and your spouse, earns reward. The Prophet, peace be upon him, even said that in a man's intimacy with his wife there is charity. The companions were surprised, and he said: if he did it in a forbidden way, wouldn't he carry a sin?

Same with your work and your studies: intend to support your family, to never need to ask people, to serve your religion where you are, and to honor your parents through your success. Be a trader of intentions! Allah says, meaning: Say, my prayer, my worship, my living and my dying are all for Allah, Lord of the worlds.

Points five and six: Actions are only by intentions, and every person will have what they intended. Not a repetition! The first is about the action itself: its type, its validity, and whether it's accepted. The second is about the reward: you get as much as you intended.

A nice benefit: there's no action without an intention. So the person who keeps worrying, did I really intend to fast or not? while he got up for the pre-dawn meal and stirred honey into his yogurt, the answer is: you intended six hundred times! Intention isn't some complicated yoga thing. It's the easiest thing in the world, so close the door to these doubts.

Look at the hadith's own example: whoever's migration was for Allah and His Messenger, that's the purpose. Their migration is for Allah and His Messenger, that's the reward. And even if two people pray for Allah, their reward isn't the same: one is focused, one is in tears, and one adds more good intentions to the same prayer.

When intention goes wrong

And see how serious intention is in a hadith in Sahih Muslim. Among the first judged on the Day of Judgment: a man who died fighting in Allah's cause. Allah asks him: what did you do? He says: I fought for You until I died. He's told: You lied. You fought so people would call you brave, and they did. And he's thrown into the Fire!

The second: a man who learned knowledge, taught it, and recited the Quran. He's told: you learned so people would call you a scholar, and recited so they'd call you a reciter, and they did. The third: a man Allah gave wealth, and he spent it. He's told: you did it so people would call you generous, and they did.

That's why, when people say: do good and good will come back to you, because of the universe and karma, no. Good comes back to you because Allah said so. If your intention is this world, you'll get it in this world only. And if your intention is the Hereafter, Allah will honor you in both.

And the opposite: the Prophet, peace be upon him, said: You will never spend anything seeking Allah's pleasure except that you'll be rewarded for it, even the bite of food you put in your wife's mouth. So even that bite, if you intend it for Allah, has reward.

Conditions of acceptance

Point seven: three conditions for any deed to be accepted. First, tawhid: the person doing it believes in Allah alone. Second, sincerity: the deed is for Allah. Third, following the Prophet's way, peace be upon him, meaning it's not an innovation in religion.

A person without faith, even if they give charity with a good heart, it isn't accepted as worship, and Allah says, meaning: nothing prevented their spending from being accepted except that they disbelieved in Allah and His Messenger.

Al-Fudayl ibn Iyad explained the verse: so He may test which of you is best in deeds. He said: the best deed is the most sincere and the most correct. Most sincere: done for Allah. Most correct: done according to the Sunnah. If it's sincere but not correct, it's not accepted. And if it's correct but not sincere, it's not accepted either.

So if someone tells you: say this phrase exactly eleven hundred and fifty-three times and such and such will happen to you, ask: where's the evidence? If there's nothing from the Quran or the Sunnah, it's an innovation, even if your intention is good. And the danger is that you start believing the number is what helps you, not Allah.

Showing off

Point eight: mixing showing off into your intention, called riya'. It has three forms. First: the deed is for people from the very start, and it's lost. Second: from the start it's for Allah and for people together, and it's also rejected. Third: it starts for Allah, then showing off creeps in.

In a hadith qudsi, Allah says: I am the One most free of need of any partner. Whoever does a deed associating someone else with Me, I leave him with his partner. So Allah doesn't accept a partner: either it's all for Him, or it counts for nothing.

What about the third form? If the thought comes and you push it away and hate it, your deed is accepted, and you're even rewarded for resisting. But if you go along with it, and add to your deed for people, then the stronger view is that you keep the reward of the part done for Allah, and lose the part done to show off.

And these whispers are like little kids throwing pebbles at a train. If the train keeps going, the pebbles do nothing. If it stops and gets off to deal with them, that's exactly what they want! The Prophet, peace be upon him, said: Praise be to Allah, who reduced his plot to mere whispering. And don't feel guilty about whispers you push away. That's not a sin, it's a good deed.

Worldly benefits

Point nine: adding a worldly benefit to your intention. You want Allah's pleasure, along with a permissible worldly benefit, like doing Hajj and also trading, teaching Quran for a salary, or seeking forgiveness so Allah provides for you. This is allowed, and it's not showing off, because you're asking this world from Allah.

The evidence: about Hajj, Allah says, meaning: there's no blame on you for seeking bounty from your Lord. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said: the most deserving thing to take a wage for is the Book of Allah. And he said: whoever would like his provision to be expanded and his life to be extended, let him keep ties with his relatives.

But it does affect the reward. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that any army that fights in Allah's cause and gains spoils has already taken two-thirds of its reward in this world, and one-third remains. So the person who teaches Quran for Allah's sake for free gets a fuller reward than the one who takes a salary.

There's a difference: some deeds exist only for Allah, like prayer, fasting and supplication, and they're only valid if done for Him. Other deeds are worldly by nature, like studying for a degree, working for a salary, or playing sports. They're not sinful if you do them for this world, but you lose a lot if you don't add an intention for Allah. That's the scholars' trade.

Key rules

Point twelve: a good intention doesn't fix a bad deed. Like someone who steals to give charity, or sings in a nightclub to save money for Hajj! Another example: pirated courses sold online. You say, I'm learning something useful to earn a halal income, but the foundation is forbidden.

Point thirteen: intention alone isn't enough if you're able to do the deed. Someone who says, what matters is that my heart prays, while he's able to pray and doesn't, his intention isn't truly sincere, because a sincere intention shows in effort.

Point fourteen: intention is enough when you truly can't. Like the companions at the battle of Tabuk who had no mounts to ride, and went back with tears in their eyes. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said: there are people in Medina who were with you on every journey and every valley you crossed; only their excuse held them back.

In a hadith in at-Tirmidhi: this world is for four kinds of people. One has wealth and knowledge, and does good with them. One has knowledge but no wealth, and says, if I had money I'd do the same; they're equal in reward. One has wealth but no knowledge, and spends it on sin. And one has neither, and wishes to be like him; they're equal in sin.

Be careful: many young people scroll through TikTok wishing they were like people doing haram things, and girls wishing to be like influencers doing wrong. That wish can carry the same sin. So intend the good you can't reach instead.

But there's a difference: whoever intends a bad deed, then leaves it for Allah while able, gets a good deed written. But whoever was on his way to do it, and simply couldn't, carries its sin. Like the hadith: when two Muslims meet with their swords, both the killer and the killed are in the Fire, because the one killed was eager to kill the other.

Point fifteen: a passing thought isn't an intention. Thoughts that cross your mind, good or bad, aren't counted as long as you push them away. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said: Allah has overlooked for my nation what their minds whisper to them, as long as they don't act on it or speak of it.

Point sixteen: it's enough to intend a deed for Allah, even if you don't know its exact reward. And the opposite: whoever knows something is forbidden carries its sin, even if they didn't know how big that sin was.

Finally: do we say our intention out loud, like, I intend to pray the noon prayer? This wasn't reported from the Prophet, his companions, or the generation after them, and the stronger view is that it's an innovation that opens the door to obsessive doubts. Intention lives in the heart. As for saying Labbayk for Umrah, that's the pilgrim's call, a public rite, not saying your intention out loud.

And at the end of the hadith, notice the difference: for Allah and His Messenger, he repeated the words to honor it. But for worldly aims, he only said: their migration is for what they migrated for, as if to make it small. And the migration that's open to all of us: a true emigrant is the one who leaves what Allah has forbidden.

Review & practice

Let's review: intention is the purpose, it makes a deed valid and accepted, and your reward depends on it. It separates worship from habit, and turns everyday things into worship. The conditions of acceptance are tawhid, sincerity and following the Sunnah. Adding a worldly benefit is allowed, showing off is not. And a sincere intention is enough when you truly can't.

Your practice this week: pick something ordinary you do every day, like eating, working or studying, and write down three intentions for it. Before any act of worship, ask yourself: who am I doing this for? And ask Allah to bless you with sincerity in your words and deeds.
