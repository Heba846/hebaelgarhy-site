What I Have Forbidden You, Avoid

Avoiding the forbidden, doing what you can, and the etiquette of asking

A simple explanation of the hadith “What I have forbidden you, avoid it; and what I have commanded you, do as much of it as you can”, the ninth hadith in Imam an-Nawawi's Forty Hadith. Why must we avoid all of the forbidden but do commands as far as we are able? Learn about necessity, why leaving sins is greater than extra good deeds, the authority of the Sunnah, “hardship brings ease”, and the kinds of questions the Prophet (peace be upon him) discouraged.

The hadith

عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ عَبْدِ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنِ صَخْرٍ رَضِيَ اللهُ عَنْهُ قَالَ: سَمِعْت رَسُولَ اللَّهِ صلى الله عليه و سلم يَقُولُ: "مَا نَهَيْتُكُمْ عَنْهُ فَاجْتَنِبُوهُ، وَمَا أَمَرْتُكُمْ بِهِ فَأْتُوا مِنْهُ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُمْ، فَإِنَّمَا أَهْلَكَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ كَثْرَةُ مَسَائِلِهِمْ وَاخْتِلَافُهُمْ عَلَى أَنْبِيَائِهِمْ ".

I heard the Messenger of Allah (ﷺ) say, “What I have forbidden for you, avoid. What I have ordered you who were before you.”

Reported by al-Bukhari and Muslim — an-Nawawi's Forty Hadith, no. 9

The hadith & its narrator

Assalamu alaikum, I'm Heba. If this is your first time here: I'm summarizing Imam an-Nawawi's Forty Hadith, one hadith at a time, using mind maps and simple explanations, for anyone who doesn't have time for long lessons. And remember: what cannot be done completely should not be left completely. So let's begin: hadith number nine.

Why, with forbidden things, are we told: avoid them, all of them, but with commands: do as much as you are able? And why did the Prophet, peace be upon him, forbid certain kinds of questions? That's today's hadith, the ninth.

عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ عَبْدِ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنِ صَخْرٍ رَضِيَ اللهُ عَنْهُ قَالَ: سَمِعْت رَسُولَ اللَّهِ صلى الله عليه و سلم يَقُولُ: "مَا نَهَيْتُكُمْ عَنْهُ فَاجْتَنِبُوهُ، وَمَا أَمَرْتُكُمْ بِهِ فَأْتُوا مِنْهُ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُمْ، فَإِنَّمَا أَهْلَكَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ كَثْرَةُ مَسَائِلِهِمْ وَاخْتِلَافُهُمْ عَلَى أَنْبِيَائِهِمْ

From Abu Hurayrah, Abd ar-Rahman ibn Sakhr, may Allah be pleased with him, who said: I heard the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, say: What I have forbidden you, avoid it. And what I have commanded you, do as much of it as you are able. For what destroyed those before you was only their many questions and their disagreement with their prophets.

The narrator is Abu Hurayrah, whose name was Abd ar-Rahman ibn Sakhr, may Allah be pleased with him. And the hadith is agreed upon, meaning it's in both al-Bukhari and Muslim.

“Avoid it”

Point one: “What I have forbidden you, avoid it.” You must stay away from everything forbidden, whatever your ability. Why? Because leaving something takes no effort: you don't need to do anything in order not to do something forbidden. And most forbidden things don't even cross our minds.

So no one can open the door to something forbidden with the excuse: I can't help it. The lesson recalls the young man who came to the Prophet, peace be upon him, asking permission for zina, unlawful sexual relations. The Prophet didn't tell him: do as you like. He convinced him through conversation, asking: Would you like it for your mother? Then he prayed for him, and the young man left hating zina.

What about necessity? Necessity makes the forbidden permissible, but only as much as needed. Necessity means facing death, or real harm, not just a desire. Allah says, meaning: whoever is forced by necessity, neither desiring it nor going beyond the limit, there is no sin on him. And from this comes the famous rule: necessities make the forbidden permissible.

“As much as you are able”

As for commands: “do as much of it as you are able”, because doing something takes effort. The lesson's example: if someone tells you to pray ten units, you might answer: how about eight? Here, ability counts. And even obligations themselves depend on ability.

Like the hadith, meaning: Pray standing; if you can't, then sitting; and if you can't, then on your side. The sick and the traveler have a concession in fasting. And Hajj is due on whoever is able to find a way to it.

So which is better: doing an obligation, or leaving the forbidden? This takes us back to the three types of patience: patience in obeying Allah, patience in staying away from sin, and patience with what Allah decrees. Patience with the decree is the easiest, because there's no choice in it. And one view says doing acts of obedience is better, because they depend on ability, so they're harder.

But leaving the forbidden is better than doing recommended acts. In a hadith qudsi, Allah says, meaning: My servant does not draw near to Me with anything more beloved to Me than what I have made obligatory on him. And one of the early Muslims said: good deeds are done by both the righteous and the sinful, but leaving sins, only a siddiq, a person of deep truthfulness, can do that.

The lesson's example: in Ramadan, the night prayers, tarawih, are packed. Everyone prays. But leaving backbiting, or quitting cigarettes, only a few manage that. And beware of being deceived: doing lots of recommended acts while you're still persisting in something forbidden.

Aishah, may Allah be pleased with her, said: whoever would love to outrun the tireless worshipper, let him hold back from sins. And the Prophet, peace be upon him, said, meaning: Guard against what is forbidden, and you will be the most devoted worshipper among people.

The Sunnah is binding

Another point: the default for the Prophet's commands, peace be upon him, is that they're obligatory. The evidence: he said, meaning: If it weren't that I'd make it hard on my nation, I would have commanded them to use the siwak, the tooth-stick. So, had he commanded it, the siwak would have become obligatory.

And the hadith confirms that the Sunnah is a binding source of law. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said: Indeed, I have been given the Quran, and something like it along with it. The Sunnah can establish a ruling on its own, like forbidding domestic donkeys, and every predator with fangs. And it can explain the Quran, or narrow down a general ruling in it.

وَمَا آتَاكُمُ الرَّسُولُ فَخُذُوهُ وَمَا نَهَاكُمْ عَنْهُ فَانْتَهُوا

Whatever the Messenger gives you, take it; and whatever he forbids you, refrain from it.

Rules of ease

From this hadith comes a rule: hardship brings ease. But this doesn't mean the normal hardship that comes with worship, like hunger while fasting, or the heat on your way to the mosque. It means hardship that can't be endured. And hardship is relative: it differs from person to person.

Another rule: what's possible isn't dropped because of what's impossible. So someone who can't prostrate still stands and bows as normal, and only sits for the prostration. He doesn't leave everything because one part is hard for him.

The etiquette of asking

And the end of the hadith: what destroyed those before you was their many questions and their disagreement with their prophets. The prophets are protected from error in conveying the message. And Umar, may Allah be pleased with him, when he objected on the day of al-Hudaybiyah, later said: I did many deeds because of that. Meaning, he did many good deeds to make up for it.

One form of disagreeing with the prophets is opposing the Prophet's words with your own opinion. Look at the manners of al-Hubab ibn al-Mundhir at Badr: first he asked, is this revelation, or opinion? And when he learned it was opinion, he suggested another place. Another form: a question that causes something to become forbidden when it wasn't. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said, meaning: the Muslim with the greatest crime is the one who asks about something that wasn't forbidden, and it becomes forbidden because of his question.

So which questions are forbidden? The lesson lists seven. First: asking about something Allah left unmentioned while revelation was coming down, like the man who asked about Hajj: every year? And the Prophet, peace be upon him, said: Leave me as long as I leave you. Second: stubbornness and stirring up doubts, like those who said: show us Allah openly.

Third: asking about what we have no way of knowing, like when the Hour will come, or how Allah's attributes are. Imam Malik said: Allah's rising over the Throne, the istiwa', is known, but how it is, is unknown. Fourth: a beginner asking about tiny side details before the basics.

Fifth: a question that shows weak submission, like the cow of the Children of Israel. Allah told them to slaughter a cow, and they kept asking: what color? What kind? They made it hard, so Allah made it hard on them. Sixth: a question with no practical use, like: if we travel to Mars, how will we pray? And seventh: a question meant to embarrass the person being asked.

But note: a question that suits your level, so you can learn and act, is very good and encouraged. The problem is a question with no purpose, or with a wrong purpose.

Review & practice

Let's review: we avoid all that's forbidden, and do commands as much as we're able. Necessity permits the forbidden, only as much as needed. Leaving the forbidden is better than recommended acts. The Sunnah is binding. And stay away from pointless questions, or ones meant to be stubborn.

Your practice this week: pick one forbidden thing you keep doing, leave it for Allah, and don't say: I can't. Do acts of obedience as much as you can, even if it's a little. And before you ask a question, ask yourself: how will this benefit me?
