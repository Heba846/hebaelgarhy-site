The Halal Is Clear, and the Haram Is Clear

Halal, haram, and the doubtful matters in between

A simple explanation of the hadith “The halal is clear and the haram is clear”, the sixth hadith in Imam an-Nawawi's Forty Hadith. Why do doubtful matters exist? Learn about definitive and speculative evidence, how a scholar, a student and an ordinary Muslim should deal with differences of opinion, acceptable vs. unacceptable disagreement, careful piety (wara') vs. extremism, the shepherd near the protected pasture, and the heart as the king of the body.

The hadith

عَنْ أَبِي عَبْدِ اللَّهِ النُّعْمَانِ بْنِ بَشِيرٍ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُمَا، قَالَ: سَمِعْت رَسُولَ اللَّهِ صلى الله عليه و سلم يَقُولُ: "إنَّ الْحَلَالَ بَيِّنٌ، وَإِنَّ الْحَرَامَ بَيِّنٌ، وَبَيْنَهُمَا أُمُورٌ مُشْتَبِهَاتٌ لَا يَعْلَمُهُنَّ كَثِيرٌ مِنْ النَّاسِ، فَمَنْ اتَّقَى الشُّبُهَاتِ فَقْد اسْتَبْرَأَ لِدِينِهِ وَعِرْضِهِ، وَمَنْ وَقَعَ فِي الشُّبُهَاتِ وَقَعَ فِي الْحَرَامِ، كَالرَّاعِي يَرْعَى حَوْلَ الْحِمَى يُوشِكُ أَنْ يَرْتَعَ فِيهِ، أَلَا وَإِنَّ لِكُلِّ مَلِكٍ حِمًى، أَلَا وَإِنَّ حِمَى اللَّهِ مَحَارِمُهُ، أَلَا وَإِنَّ فِي الْجَسَدِ مُضْغَةً إذَا صَلَحَتْ صَلَحَ الْجَسَدُ كُلُّهُ، وَإذَا فَسَدَتْ فَسَدَ الْجَسَدُ كُلُّهُ، أَلَا وَهِيَ الْقَلْبُ". .

I heard the Messenger of Allah (ﷺ) say, “That which is lawful is clear and that which is unlawful is clear, and between the two of them are doubtful matters about which many people do not know. Thus he who avoids doubtful matters clears himself in regard to his religion and his honor, but he who falls into doubtful matters is diseased. Truly, it is the heart.”

Reported by al-Bukhari and Muslim — an-Nawawi's Forty Hadith, no. 6

The hadith & its narrator

Assalamu alaikum, I'm Heba. If this is your first time here: I'm summarizing Imam an-Nawawi's Forty Hadith, one hadith at a time, using mind maps and simple explanations, for anyone who doesn't have time for long lessons. And remember: what cannot be done completely should not be left completely. So let's begin: hadith number six.

Have you ever stood in front of something and not known: is this halal or haram, allowed or forbidden? One shaykh says it's halal, another says it's haram, and you're left confused! Hadith number six gives you a full map for this confusion, and tells you what to do.

This hadith is one of the three that Imam Ahmad said Islam revolves around: Actions are only by intentions; Whoever introduces something into this matter of ours that is not from it; and today's hadith: The halal is clear and the haram is clear, which covers the details of Islam's laws.

The narrator is an-Nu'man ibn Bashir, may Allah be pleased with them both. Why do we say 'them both' and not just 'him'? Because he and his father were both companions of the Prophet. The same goes for Abdullah ibn Umar and Abdullah ibn Amr, may Allah be pleased with them both.

عَنْ أَبِي عَبْدِ اللَّهِ النُّعْمَانِ بْنِ بَشِيرٍ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُمَا، قَالَ: سَمِعْت رَسُولَ اللَّهِ صلى الله عليه و سلم يَقُولُ: "إنَّ الْحَلَالَ بَيِّنٌ، وَإِنَّ الْحَرَامَ بَيِّنٌ، وَبَيْنَهُمَا أُمُورٌ مُشْتَبِهَاتٌ لَا يَعْلَمُهُنَّ كَثِيرٌ مِنْ النَّاسِ

From Abu Abdullah an-Nu'man ibn Bashir, may Allah be pleased with them both, who said: I heard the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, say: The halal is clear, and the haram is clear, and between them are doubtful matters that many people do not know.

فَمَنْ اتَّقَى الشُّبُهَاتِ فَقْد اسْتَبْرَأَ لِدِينِهِ وَعِرْضِهِ، وَمَنْ وَقَعَ فِي الشُّبُهَاتِ وَقَعَ فِي الْحَرَامِ، كَالرَّاعِي يَرْعَى حَوْلَ الْحِمَى يُوشِكُ أَنْ يَرْتَعَ فِيهِ

So whoever guards against the doubtful matters has protected his religion and his honor. And whoever falls into the doubtful matters falls into the haram, like a shepherd grazing around a protected pasture, who is about to graze inside it.

أَلَا وَإِنَّ لِكُلِّ مَلِكٍ حِمًى، أَلَا وَإِنَّ حِمَى اللَّهِ مَحَارِمُهُ، أَلَا وَإِنَّ فِي الْجَسَدِ مُضْغَةً إذَا صَلَحَتْ صَلَحَ الْجَسَدُ كُلُّهُ، وَإذَا فَسَدَتْ فَسَدَ الْجَسَدُ كُلُّهُ، أَلَا وَهِيَ الْقَلْبُ

Truly, every king has a protected pasture, and truly, Allah's protected pasture is what He has forbidden. Truly, in the body there is a piece of flesh: if it is sound, the whole body is sound, and if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt. Truly, it is the heart.

Clear and doubtful

First: what does 'clear' mean, and what does 'doubtful' mean? Clear means obvious: the scholar and the layperson both know it, like the obligation of prayer and fasting Ramadan, and the prohibition of adultery, alcohol, and interest, called riba. Doubtful means its ruling is hidden from many people, so they don't know: is it halal or haram?

And sometimes the doubt isn't only about halal and haram, but about the level too: is this obligatory, or just recommended? Is it disliked, or forbidden? And notice the words 'many people': not all people. Some people know the ruling well.

Why is there doubt?

So why are there doubtful matters at all? In the lesson, we learn that doubt comes from four causes: the evidence itself is speculative, not definitive; or the person never received the evidence; or he received it but didn't understand it, because his knowledge is limited; or he didn't understand it, because his intention isn't sound.

To understand what 'speculative evidence' means, we need to understand its opposite: definitive evidence. And evidence can be definitive in two ways. Definitive in its transmission: there's no doubt it reached us correctly. And definitive in its meaning: it has only one meaning, and no one can read it any other way.

An example of definitive transmission: the entire Quran, because it reached us through mass transmission, called tawatur, with thousands of chains. And also the mass-transmitted hadith: one chain coming from Syria, one from the Hijaz, one from Yemen, people who never met, and they all said the same thing! It's impossible for them to have agreed on a lie.

And the hadiths of al-Bukhari and Muslim, even the ones that aren't mass-transmitted, were received by the Muslim nation with acceptance, because those two imams were extremely strict in choosing hadiths. So they became close to definitive in their authenticity.

An example of definitive meaning: Allah's words, meaning: for the male, a share equal to that of two females. No one can understand it any other way. And Hajj: a companion asked the Prophet, peace be upon him: Every year? He said: If I said yes, it would become obligatory, and you would not be able to do it. So Hajj is obligatory once in a lifetime, and that's very clear.

And speculative evidence? Also two types. The first: speculative in transmission, like a hadith that comes through only one or two routes. Here the hadith scholar has to check: is the chain connected? Are the narrators trustworthy, with good memory? And two scholars may differ: one says this narrator is trustworthy, the other says he's weak. So one accepts the hadith, and the other rejects it.

And who has the doubt here? You and me, not the scholar. The scholar has settled the matter: for him, the hadith is either authentic or weak. But we hear one shaykh say 'Sunnah' and another say 'innovation', about things like Salat at-Tasbih, reciting Surat al-Kahf on Friday, or the night of mid-Sha'ban, and we get confused.

The second type: speculative in meaning. The verse or hadith is established for everyone, but it can be understood in more than one way. Like the verse, meaning: and let them draw their head-coverings over their chests. One scholar understood from it that the face veil, the niqab, is obligatory, and another understood that it's recommended. The issue here isn't the transmission, it's the understanding.

That's why you find the same hadith in all the books, and ash-Shafi'i understood it one way, while Malik understood it another way. And here too, the doubt is with the person who doesn't understand the reason for the disagreement, not with the scholar who understood the evidence and settled it.

The wisdom behind doubt

So why didn't Allah make the whole religion clear: halal obvious, haram obvious, and that's it? In the lesson, we learn that speculative evidence is actually one of the strongest reasons the religion itself has been preserved! How?

Imagine if every ruling were written out explicitly: you open the book, go to page such-and-such, and find the ruling. Then the scholar and the ignorant would be the same, and no one would work hard to seek knowledge. But speculative evidence made scholars learn the Arabic language, the principles of jurisprudence, the science of hadith, and the rules of Quran interpretation, to reach Allah's ruling.

And when memorization comes together with understanding, no one can distort the religion. That's why, as soon as someone comes up with a strange understanding that damages the religion, you find scholars responding the very next day: this understanding goes against the early generations, the salaf, and the consensus of the Muslim nation.

The second wisdom: testing. In what's clear, Allah tests us in one thing: following, doing the halal and leaving the haram. But in what's doubtful, there are two tests: reaching Allah's ruling without following your desires, and then following it.

So if scholars differ, are they all right? No. Only one of them is right, and only Allah knows who. But they're all rewarded: whoever does his best, called ijtihad, and gets it right has two rewards, and whoever does his best and gets it wrong has one reward, and he won't be blamed on the Day of Judgment.

Where do we stand?

People facing this doubt are of three kinds, and each one is tested in his own way: the mujtahid scholar, who derives rulings himself; the advanced student of knowledge; and the ordinary person who follows a scholar, called a muqallid. And notice: this talk about ijtihad is for the scholar who has the tools, not for you and me!

The mujtahid scholar is tested in three things. Gaining the tools of ijtihad, like Arabic, the principles of jurisprudence, and the science of hadith. Working hard and putting real effort into research, without taking shortcuts. And freeing himself from his desires, not starting the research already wanting the result to match what he likes.

And even after reaching a view, he keeps learning, and he may change it. When Imam ash-Shafi'i came to Egypt, he changed many of his views. And the reward for this effort is beyond compare. Allah said:

يَرْفَعِ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مِنْكُمْ وَالَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ دَرَجَاتٍ

Allah will raise those of you who believe, and those who have been given knowledge, by many degrees.

Imam Ibn al-Qayyim named his book about giving religious rulings, or fatwas: Informing Those Who Sign on Behalf of the Lord of the Worlds. In other words, whoever says halal and haram is as if he's signing on Allah's behalf! That's why the companions used to pass fatwas on to each other, and Umar would gather the people of Badr for a major issue.

And sadly, the easiest thing for us is to say: 'it looks haram', or 'come on, it's fine'. Well, what does haram look like? And is there even a ruling called 'fine'? Talking about religion has to be done carefully.

The second kind: the advanced student of knowledge. He's below the mujtahid: he knows the scholars' views and their evidence, and can weigh between them, but doesn't come up with a new view of his own. And imagine: Imam an-Nawawi himself, the author of the Forty, was at this level! In his book al-Majmu', he compares the schools of law and weighs between them.

As for the true mujtahids, they're people like Imam Ahmad, ash-Shafi'i, Malik, Sufyan ath-Thawri, Ibn Taymiyyah, and Ibn al-Qayyim. And in the lesson, it's said that roughly ninety-nine percent of us are followers, just so we know our level, and don't rush to think of ourselves as scholars.

And a scholar can be a mujtahid in one field and not in another. You'll hear: so-and-so is strong in hadith and weak in jurisprudence, and that's completely normal. And the test for the student of knowledge is not to be fanatical about his school or his teacher, and to choose by evidence, not by desire.

The third kind: the layperson who follows, and that's most of us. His role is to ask a scholar and follow his view. And his test is in choosing the right scholar, on three foundations: known for his knowledge; known for his religion and wara', careful piety; and if the issue is new, someone who understands today's reality.

And once you've chosen, take his view, whether you like it or not. But if you keep going around from shaykh to shaykh until you find one who tells you what makes you comfortable, then, as the lesson puts it: you're following yourself, not a scholar!

Acceptable disagreement

So does this doubt mean the religion is incomplete, or lost? Not at all. The hadith said 'many people', not all people, so there are always people who know the truth. And the Prophet, peace be upon him, said: A group of my nation will always remain upon the truth, prevailing.

From here, we can tell apart two kinds of disagreement. Acceptable disagreement is based on evidence that's speculative in transmission or meaning, like where to place your hands after rising from bowing, or whether the niqab is obligatory or recommended. We deal with it calmly; at most, we explain our view to each other. Unacceptable disagreement is an odd opinion that clashes with a definitive text, or with the consensus of the Muslim nation, and that one is rejected.

In acceptable disagreement, the lesson quotes Imam ash-Shafi'i: My opinion is right but may be wrong, and someone else's opinion is wrong but may be right. And one scholar used to say: I never entered a debate except wishing the truth would come out with the person in front of me.

An example of unacceptable disagreement: someone claiming that inheritance shares must change in modern times, even if he holds a doctorate in Islamic law, even though Allah said, in a text whose meaning is definitive:

يُوصِيكُمُ اللَّهُ فِي أَوْلَادِكُمْ لِلذَّكَرِ مِثْلُ حَظِّ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ

Allah instructs you concerning your children: for the male, a share equal to that of two females.

But before you object to someone, first make sure the issue really isn't an acceptable disagreement, and ask your shaykh. Like Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, when he saw circles of people doing dhikr, the remembrance of Allah, in the mosque: he didn't object right away. He went and asked Ibn Mas'ud first.

Now back to the other causes of doubt. Some people never received the evidence at all. Some try to understand the evidence with a good intention, but without knowledge, so they reach the wrong conclusion. And some have an unsound intention: they want to prove an idea in their head, so they twist the evidence until it says what they want.

Guarding against doubt

Now to the words: whoever guards against the doubtful matters has protected his religion and his honor. This means it's recommended to step out of the disagreement. If something might be disliked or forbidden, stay away from it as if it's forbidden. And if something might be recommended or obligatory, do it as much as you can, as if it's obligatory.

An example from the lesson: the niqab. A woman who can wear it, wears it. If it turns out to be obligatory, praise be to Allah, we did the obligation. And if it turns out to be recommended, we lost nothing and gained reward. She also spares herself people's talk, and that's the meaning of 'and his honor'.

But notice: this is wara', careful piety. It's recommended, not required. Whoever asked and learned that something is only recommended doesn't have to do it. But whoever concludes that it's obligatory has to commit to it.

Then: and whoever falls into the doubtful matters falls into the haram. What does that mean? The Prophet, peace be upon him, explained it with an example. The hima is a protected pasture, full of crops and food, and the king forbids anyone from entering it. And a shepherd leaves the whole wide world, and grazes his sheep right on its border, with no fence! At some point, the sheep will surely go in.

So 'falls into the haram' here means: comes very close to falling into it. That's why it's recommended to put some distance between yourself and the haram. That distance isn't forbidden in itself, but it protects you. And this phrase is the basis of a major principle called blocking the means, sadd adh-dhara'i': whatever leads you to the haram, close it off.

And the lesson gives examples from our lives: a young man choosing the empty street instead of the street full of girls, even though walking down a street isn't haram. Websites and pages that start out normal and end up haram. And a relationship that started with one innocent word. All of this began with circling around the hima.

But there's a big difference between wara' and going to extremes, called tanattu'. Wara' is leaving something the evidence itself shows is doubtful, like filtering your words about someone, out of fear of backbiting. Going to extremes is leaving something with no evidence that it's doubtful, like leaving your prayer because you feel something came out of you, or repeating your wudu, your ablution, over and over. And the Prophet, peace be upon him, said: The extremists are ruined.

On the other hand, people are asked to think well of others. If you see your brother doing something different from you, don't accuse him in his religion or his honor. Say: maybe he has a fatwa, maybe the evidence never reached him. And ask first, before you attack him.

The heart

And the end of the hadith: Truly, in the body there is a piece of flesh… truly, it is the heart. The Prophet, peace be upon him, mentioned the heart after talking about the king and the protected pasture, because the heart is the king of the limbs: if it's sound, all the limbs are sound, and if it's corrupt, they're corrupt.

So can someone have a corrupt heart but look good on the outside? Yes, like the hypocrite, who puts on a show of goodness in front of people. But the opposite is impossible: a sound heart with corrupt actions. So if someone tells you: he does a lot of wrong things, but his heart is pure, tell him: that's not possible. Every vessel pours out what's inside it.

That's why you should focus on the king, not just the soldiers. Clean your heart of shirk, associating partners with Allah, and of envy, showing off, and self-admiration. And fill it with faith, love of Allah, hope, trust in Allah, and thinking well of people. Then the limbs will follow the king on their own.

Review & practice

Let's review: the halal is clear and the haram is clear, and between them are doubtful matters. Doubt comes from speculative evidence, not knowing the evidence, limited understanding, or a bad intention. The layperson chooses a scholar he trusts, and follows him. Guard against doubtful matters, but without going to extremes. And most importantly: the heart.

Your practice this week: if you're unsure about something, ask one scholar you trust, and follow what he says. Look at which hima you're circling around, and stay away from it. And don't accuse anyone over an issue where scholars differ.
